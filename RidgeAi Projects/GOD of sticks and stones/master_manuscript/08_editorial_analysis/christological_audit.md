# CHRISTOLOGICAL AUDIT
## The God of Sticks and Stones — Literary and Structural Analysis
## Scope: Full Manuscript (Review Edition + Chapter 18 + Closing Reflection)

---

## SECTION ONE: COMPLETE INVENTORY OF EXPLICIT CHRIST REFERENCES

### Terminology Used in the Manuscript

**Names and titles that appear:**
- **"Christ"** — appears approximately 85–90 times across 18 chapters. By far the dominant designation.
- **"Jesus"** — appears **once**. Chapter Six, in the sentence: *"Jesus speaks with a Samaritan woman about living water."* This is the only use of the personal name in the entire body of the manuscript.
- **"the carpenter"** — appears as a recurring designation, primarily in Chapter Ten.
- **"the Good Shepherd"** — applied as a self-identification in Chapter Seven.
- **"the cornerstone" / "rejected stone"** — applied to Christ, repeated across Chapters Three, Thirteen, Eighteen.
- **"the true vine"** — implicit in the quotation *"I am the vine"* (Chapter Two).
- **"bread of life"** — named explicitly in Chapter Eighteen's title convergence.
- **"living water"** — recurring, attributed to Christ in Chapters Six, Sixteen, Eighteen.

**Names and titles that do not appear:**
- "Jesus" — once only (see above)
- "the Son" (as theological title) — never
- "Messiah" — never
- "Lamb" — never
- "Word made flesh" / "Logos" — never
- "Lord" — never in the body chapters as applied to Christ
- "Savior" — never
- "Incarnation" — never
- "Redeemer" — never

### Chapter-by-Chapter Inventory

**Preface**
- "Christ as the fulfillment toward which all these symbolic movements quietly point."
— Sole Christ reference. Functions as a compass bearing for the whole manuscript.

**Introduction**
- "The patterns continue until they converge fully in Christ. / The cornerstone. The true vine. The bread of life. The living water. The shepherd."
— First explicit Christ passage of substance. Announces the symbolic convergence.
- "Christ descends before resurrection."
— Pattern statement; biographical detail embedded in list.

**Chapter One — Dust and Breath**
- "Christ Himself enters the world not through imperial spectacle, but through dust, wood, water, bread, and burial."
- "Even Christ often taught through seeds, trees, soil, weather, and labor."
- "He walks through fields. Touches soil. Spits into dust to restore sight. Rides into cities on borrowed animals. Washes feet. Breaks bread. Sleeps in storms. Dies upon wood. Rises within a garden."
- "And perhaps this is also why Christ spent so little time among monuments."
— Four references. All concern Christ's earthly movement and method, not his person or identity.

**Chapter Two — The Tree and the Garden**
- "Creation was never silent. Humanity simply became difficult to quiet." [chapter epigraph — not a Christ reference but precedes the tree-cross link]
- "Christ Himself repeatedly teaches through vineyards and orchards."
- "One of scripture's most haunting moments occurs when humanity crowns Christ with wood."
- "Christ does not ascend the tower. He ascends the tree."
- "The risen Christ is first mistaken for a gardener."
- "Christ later says: 'I am the vine.'"
- "For most of His earthly life, He lives quietly. Working with wood. Walking dusty roads."
- "Even His triumph enters Jerusalem humbly."
— Eight references. This chapter contains the manuscript's first sustained symbolic treatment of the cross (wood/tree) and resurrection (gardener). The vine quotation is the first direct Gospel speech attributed to Christ.

**Chapter Three — Stone and Witness**
- "Even Christ says: if humanity becomes silent, the stones themselves would cry out."
— Direct Gospel quotation, Luke 19:40 (also the book's epigraph).
- "Christ later speaks directly into this reality. One builder constructs upon sand. Another upon rock."
- "This symbolic movement reaches fulfillment in Christ. He is called: cornerstone, rejected stone, stumbling stone, foundation."
- "Even the resurrection carries stone imagery."
- "Perhaps this is why Christ compares His people to living stones."
— Five references. Stone chapter is where the cornerstone title first receives extended treatment.

**Chapter Four — Seed and Field**
- "This is one reason Christ speaks so frequently through agricultural imagery."
- "A farmer scattering seed becomes a revelation of the kingdom."
- "Wheat falling into the earth becomes a picture of death and resurrection."
- "Christ says the kingdom is like seed scattered upon the earth."
- "Even Christ spends most of His earthly life in obscurity. / Thirty years largely hidden. Working. Walking. Living quietly among ordinary people."
- "Christ enters burial before resurrection."
- "And still, Christ continues speaking through farming."
- "Perhaps this is why Christ speaks so carefully about small beginnings."
— Eight references. The pattern of burial/resurrection is attributed to Christ within the seed pattern. The hiddenness of Christ's early life introduced.

**Chapter Five — Brick and Empire**
- "Christ enters directly into the center of imperial civilization, yet refuses its methods."
- "Rome builds roads. Christ walks upon them."
- "Even Christ's death reveals the contrast."
- "In Christ, heaven descends toward humanity."
— Four references. The descent-of-heaven movement introduced here directly through Christ.

**Chapter Six — Wells and Depths**
- "Perhaps this is why Christ speaks so directly about thirst. 'If anyone thirsts, let him come to Me and drink.'"
— Direct quotation (John 7:37).
- **"At Jacob's well, Jesus speaks with a Samaritan woman about living water."**
— The manuscript's only use of "Jesus." The personal name appears here and nowhere else.
- "But Christ speaks about water that becomes a spring within."
- "Christ descends into death. Burial enters the earth."
- "The deepest well in scripture becomes the grave itself. And from it, life rises again."
— Five references. The Jacob's well scene receives extended treatment. The John 7:37 quotation is the only direct speech attributed to Jesus in first person in the well chapters.

**Chapter Seven — Wilderness and Shepherds**
- "Even Christ enters the wilderness before beginning public ministry."
- "Christ calls Himself the Good Shepherd."
- "Perhaps this is why Christ enters history not as emperor, but as shepherd."
- "The shepherd leaves the ninety-nine to search for one."
— Four references. The Good Shepherd passage is where Christ's self-identification as shepherd is named, though the author does not quote John 10 directly.

**Chapter Eight — Mountains, Caves, and Lower Places**
- "Christ teaches from hillsides. The sermon overlooking Galilee rises from earth itself rather than from imperial halls."
- "The transfiguration happens upon a mountain."
- "This is one reason Christ speaks so strongly about humility."
- "Christ repeatedly withdraws from spectacle. Crowds attempt to make Him king, yet He retreats into mountains."
- "Christ kneels to wash feet. / People fall before Him. / The cross lowers Him publicly. / The tomb receives Him. / Then resurrection reverses death itself."
- "Christ enters the tomb before resurrection."
— Six references. The foot-washing sequence is the most compressed personal narrative of the Passion arc.

**Chapter Nine — Temple and Tabernacle**
- "Christ Himself teaches there." [in the temple]
- "Yet even Christ repeatedly redirects attention away from structure alone."
- *"Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it again."* / "Christ speaks of His body."
— Direct Gospel quotation (John 2:19). Only the second Gospel quotation attributed to Christ in first person in the body chapters.
- "After resurrection, Christ is mistaken first for a gardener."
- "The Spirit dwells among people themselves. / Living stones. Living temple. Living body."
— Five references. The temple-body substitution is the most theologically concentrated statement in the manuscript: Christ replaces architectural sacred space with his own body.

**Chapter Ten — The Carpenter and the Cross**
*(The most Christologically dense chapter)*
- "Long before Christ carried the cross, He worked with wood."
- "The One through whom creation itself was formed enters human history through ordinary craftsmanship."
— Only place in the manuscript where Christ's pre-existence or cosmic identity is implied ("through whom creation itself was formed").
- "Christ spends decades in obscurity learning this rhythm."
- "Even when crowds gather, Christ repeatedly withdraws. Into mountains. Into wilderness. Into prayer."
- "Christ walks among ordinary laborers instead."
- The cross sequence (extended, ~400 words): Christ is referred to throughout as "the Creator," "the carpenter," "Him" — not named directly in the cross passage itself until: "The builders of Babel attempted to ascend upward toward heaven. At the cross, heaven descends fully into human suffering."
- "The tree that once symbolized grasping in Eden now becomes place of surrender."
- "The Creator enters the deepest lowered place. / Burial."
- "The carpenter who once worked quietly with wood now becomes cornerstone of an entirely new creation."
- "The risen Christ is mistaken first for a gardener."
— Twelve or more references. This chapter is the manuscript's Christological center of gravity. The cross passage is the emotional and symbolic apex of the entire work. Notably, in the actual cross passage, Christ is called "the Creator" and "Him" — not "Christ" or "Jesus." The restraint intensifies the weight.

**Chapter Eleven — Babel After Babel**
- "Christ teaches beside fields and water rather than imperial courts."
- "Christ eats with sinners. Touches lepers. Weeps over cities. Offers water to the thirsty."
— Two references. Both characterize Christ's movement toward the marginalized.

**Chapter Twelve — The Language of Creation**
- "Perhaps this is why Christ continually teaches through ordinary created things."
- "This is why Christ repeatedly says: 'He who has ears to hear, let him hear.'"
— Two references. Both concern Christ's pedagogy.

**Chapter Thirteen — The Stone the Builders Rejected**
- "From altars built of uncut stone, to temple foundations, to memorial stones, to Christ Himself, scripture repeatedly returns to the symbolism of stone."
- *"The stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone."* — Psalm 118 quotation; Christ implied but not named.
- "This tension reaches fulfillment in Christ."
- "But Christ arrives differently. / Not as conqueror. / Not as imperial architect. / A carpenter. A servant. A living stone."
- "Christ speaks directly about this. One builder establishes upon rock. Another builds upon sand."
- "Christ therefore becomes more than teacher. He becomes reality rightly aligned."
- "The cornerstone unites heaven and earth, justice and mercy, truth and love, humility and authority."
— Seven references. This chapter extends the cornerstone identity into its fullest structural treatment.

**Chapter Fourteen — The Living Field**
- "Christ describes the kingdom as seed scattered into earth."
- "Even Christ spends decades in hiddenness before public ministry."
- "Christ warns about this directly. / Some seed falls upon hard ground."
- "Christ continually notices overlooked things: sparrows, lilies, children, widows, mustard seeds."
- "Christ repeatedly responds to humanity this way. / With patience. Mercy. Compassion."
— Five references. All concern Christ's relational posture toward humanity.

**Chapter Fifteen — The Harvest and the Laborers**
- "Christ looks upon the crowds and describes them as sheep without a shepherd."
- *"The harvest is plentiful, but the laborers are few."* — Direct Gospel quotation (Matthew 9:37).
- "This is why Christ continually moves toward people others avoid."
- "Christ sits with tax collectors. Speaks with Samaritans. Touches lepers. Forgives enemies. Washes feet."
- "Christ confronts blindness, but He also mourns over Jerusalem."
- "Christ continually compares the kingdom to ordinary labor."
- "This is why Christ compares the kingdom to leaven hidden within dough."
— Seven references. This chapter has the highest concentration of Christ's relational movement toward outsiders and the overlooked.

**Chapter Sixteen — Rivers, Wells, and Living Water**
- "Christ speaks with the Samaritan woman beside Jacob's well."
- *"Whoever drinks of the water that I shall give him will never thirst."* — Direct quotation (John 4:14).
- "Even Christ describes Himself through water. / Living water."
- "river in Genesis, living water through Christ, river restored in Revelation."
— Four references. The John 4 encounter receives its fullest treatment here.

**Chapter Seventeen — The Final City and the Restored Garden**
- "Christ descends into death before resurrection."
- "Then Christ entered history. / The veil tore. / The cornerstone appeared. / Living water flowed."
— Two references. Both are retrospective summary statements, not sustained treatment.

**Chapter Eighteen — The God of Sticks and Stones**
*(The closing chapter)*
- "Then Christ arrives. / Not as emperor. / Not as architect of empire. / A carpenter."
— The personal arrival statement; the chapter's turning point.
- "And eventually, wood becomes cross. Stone becomes tomb. Garden becomes resurrection."
- "The tree of grasping in Eden becomes the tree of surrender at Calvary."
— Only mention of "Calvary" in the manuscript.
- "Perhaps this is why Christ said that if humanity remained silent, the stones themselves would cry out."
- "Christ eats with sinners. Touches the sick. Weeps over cities. Offers living water to the thirsty."
- "This is why Christ becomes the cornerstone. / Not merely another teacher, but reality rightly aligned. / The rejected stone. The living water. The true vine. The good shepherd. The bread of life. / All the symbols converge in Him."
— The manuscript's single most explicit convergence statement. All five symbolic titles given in one passage. "Him" — not "Jesus."

**Closing Reflection — The Meeting Place**
- Zero direct Christ references. Uses "God" (three times) and "He / Him" (five times, ambiguously between God and Christ).

## SECTION TWO: WOULD A FIRST-TIME READER RECOGNIZE JESUS AS THE SUBJECT?

**The honest answer is: yes, for any reader with basic Christian literacy — but with an important limitation.**

The manuscript uses "Christ" approximately 85–90 times, beginning in the Preface, and does so consistently across all eighteen chapters. The symbolic titles (cornerstone, living water, vine, good shepherd, bread of life) are explicitly named in Chapter Eighteen. The cross, the tomb, the gardener-resurrection, the Samaritan well encounter, foot-washing, and wilderness temptation are all present. No Christian reader will finish Chapter Two without understanding who the manuscript is about.

**The limitation is this:** The manuscript presents Christ almost entirely as a theological figure rather than a person.

"Jesus" appears once. The full name — "Jesus of Nazareth" — never appears. "Messiah" never appears. "Son of God" never appears. No disciple is named. No city is named in relation to his ministry (Capernaum, Bethlehem, Nazareth — all absent). The Nativity is absent. Gethsemane is absent. The trial is absent. The Resurrection is described as pattern fulfillment (seed-burial-emergence) rather than as historical event.

For a reader with no Christian background at all — for whom "Christ" is an unfamiliar title rather than a recognized proper name — it is possible to read the manuscript as a sophisticated exploration of nature mysticism and creation theology centered on an archetypal figure called "Christ." This is not the author's intent. But the manuscript never provides the reader with the information needed to close that gap from within the text itself.

The manuscript's first-person assumption throughout is: *you already know who Christ is; I am showing you what creation has been saying about him all along.* That is a legitimate contemplative posture. But it means the manuscript is addressing initiated readers, not introducing Christ to the uninitiated.

## SECTION THREE: DOES RESTRAINT STRENGTHEN OR WEAKEN THEOLOGICAL CLARITY?

**It does both. The strengths are substantial; the single weakness is precise.**

### Where Restraint Strengthens

**Architectural integrity.** By distributing Christ references across all eighteen chapters without concentrating them in one doctrinal section, the manuscript prevents Christ from becoming a chapter-heading rather than the convergence point of the entire symbolic system. The reader arrives at Chapter Ten and Chapter Eighteen having encountered Christ in seeds, stones, wells, shepherds, trees, and fields — which means the cross and cornerstone passages carry the accumulated weight of every previous chapter. This is the manuscript's greatest structural achievement.

**Tonal consistency.** The contemplative register is maintained throughout. No chapter breaks into proclamatory mode. The manuscript never argues for Christ — it witnesses to patterns that point toward him. This is consistent with the book's central thesis: attentiveness, not argument. Restraint is not a weakness here; it is form matching content.

**Symbolic credibility.** Because the manuscript spends seventeen chapters building the language of creation before naming Christ as the convergence of that language, the reader arrives at the Christological claims having done the intellectual and emotional work. The titles in Chapter Eighteen — rejected stone, living water, true vine, good shepherd, bread of life — land with force because every symbol has been developed independently. The restraint creates the payoff.

**Literary dignity.** The decision to call Christ "the carpenter" rather than "Jesus" throughout Chapter Ten's cross passage is one of the manuscript's finest choices. It keeps the figure grounded in material reality (wood, hands, labor) while maintaining theological weight. The restraint here is not avoidance — it is precision.

### Where Restraint Weakens

**The personal name does not appear.** This is the single most significant structural gap.

"Christ" is a title — Messiah, the Anointed One. It is the correct theological category. But titles establish identity; names establish relationship. The manuscript's entire thesis moves toward communion — toward restored personal relationship between God and humanity. The Closing Reflection ends with: *"He has been walking with you."* This is deeply personal. But the person being described has no face in the manuscript. He has many titles and no name.

The Samaritan woman learned the word "Christ" from Jesus himself (John 4:25–26): *"I who speak to you am he."* The chapter describes the encounter twice (Chapters Six and Sixteen) — but she never hears his name in the manuscript. The reader doesn't either.

This creates a specific theological irony. The manuscript argues that the ordinary and particular are how God speaks — seeds, stones, wells, specific people in specific places. But the subject of the manuscript is treated as symbol rather than person. The carpenter is real. The name of the carpenter is not spoken.

This is not a doctrinal failure — it is a literary one. The manuscript is fully orthodox in what it claims about Christ. But the restraint stops precisely at the threshold where symbolic architecture would have become personal encounter. The book points toward the meeting place but names only God-in-general rather than Jesus-specifically when the reader finally arrives there.

## SECTION FOUR: THE SINGLE MOST NATURAL LOCATION FOR EXPLICIT NAMING

**Recommendation: Chapter Eighteen, at the convergence of titles.**

The passage as it currently reads:

> *"This is why Christ becomes the cornerstone. Not merely another teacher, but reality rightly aligned. The rejected stone. The living water. The true vine. The good shepherd. The bread of life. All the symbols converge in Him."*

This is the manuscript's single most explicit moment of Christological declaration. All five symbolic titles appear in one passage. It is the apex of eighteen chapters of accumulated pattern. Every seed, stone, well, tree, and field has been building toward this.

And it calls him "Him."

The most natural single intervention is not a rewrite — it is one word added at the convergence point. Something structurally equivalent to:

> *"All the symbols converge in Him. In Jesus."*

Or within the sentence architecture, something like:

> *"This is why Christ — Jesus — becomes the cornerstone."*

The parenthetical intrusion of the personal name at the moment of convergence would function as a structural key: after seventeen chapters of symbol-building, the name arrives. Not as argument. Not as proclamation. As the answer to a question the manuscript has been quietly asking since the first page.

The effect would be: the reader has been walking through a long symbolic corridor, and at the far end, a name appears. Not a title. A name. The same name the woman at the well heard when she said "I know Messiah is coming" and was told: *I who speak to you am he.*

---

### Why Not the Closing Reflection?

The Closing Reflection is addressed to the reader's post-reading experience. It serves a different function: it dissolves the boundary between the world inside the book and the reader's ordinary room. A naming of Jesus there would not be wrong — but it would arrive after the manuscript's symbolic convergence has already resolved. It would be addition rather than fulfillment.

The Closing Reflection's current anonymity ("God," "He") is also defensible as a deliberate widening: God-in-general walking with the reader, inclusive of all who encounter the ordinary world. Adding "Jesus" there makes a more specific doctrinal claim in a space designed for openness. That may or may not be the right choice — but it is a different kind of choice than placing the name at the symbolic convergence.

### Why Not Chapter Ten?

Chapter Ten's cross passage is the emotional apex of the manuscript. It is where the restraint works best. "The Creator enters the deepest lowered place" is more powerful than "Jesus enters the deepest lowered place" because the title "Creator" names what Christ is risking — not just a person dying, but the Author of creation entering death. The restraint there serves the theological weight. Breaking the anonymity at the cross would fracture the prose-poetic register at its most delicate point.

## SUMMARY FINDINGS

## FINAL STRUCTURAL OBSERVATION

The manuscript's deepest Christological insight — that Christ is the convergence point of all creation's testimony — is fully present and structurally sound. What is missing is not doctrine. What is missing is the moment of recognition that the woman at the well experienced: the moment when symbol becomes person, when "Christ" becomes "Jesus," when the meeting place stops being a metaphor and becomes a face.

The manuscript reaches the threshold of that moment in Chapter Eighteen. It then passes through it under the name "Him."

That is the one place where a single word could change everything without changing anything else.
