# Contemplative Reader Discussion Guide
## *The God of Sticks and Stones: Scripture, Creation, and the Quiet Language of God*

---

## A NOTE ON HOW TO USE THESE PROMPTS

These prompts are not debate questions. They are not designed to produce arguments, assess doctrine, or evaluate theological positions.

They are designed to slow the conversation down — the same way the book itself is designed to slow reading down.

The best discussions using this guide will be:
- Unhurried
- Observational rather than evaluative
- Personal without being confessional
- Willing to sit with an image or feeling rather than immediately resolve it

Each prompt can carry a full discussion on its own. Don't rush through the list. Choose two or three for any given session. Allow silence. Ask follow-up questions that go deeper rather than wider.

---

## OPENING PROMPTS
*Before discussing the book's content, begin here*

1. Before you read this book, what ordinary created things — if any — carried particular meaning for you? (A place in nature. A recurring season. A tree, a river, a field.) What is it about those things that stayed with you?

2. How did you experience the book's pace? Did you find yourself slowing down, or resisting the slowness? What does your experience of the pace tell you about your own interior rhythms?

3. The book was written for people who are "weary of noise and still hope to hear the quiet things again." Do you recognize yourself in that description? What does that weariness feel like in your own life?

4. What image or phrase from the manuscript stayed with you after you finished? Where did you first notice it settling?

---

## PART I — FOUNDATIONS OF CREATION (Chapters 1–3)

### Chapter One — Dust and Breath

5. The book says the kingdom of God "rarely enters history through the places humanity expects — it appears in lowered places, hidden places, places close to dust." Does this match your experience of how God has moved in your own life? What does it challenge?

6. "Creation never stopped speaking." What part of creation, if any, have you found it most difficult to hear recently? What has made it harder to hear?

7. The manuscript describes a slow drift — from the ground, from seasons, from silence — without placing blame. Do you feel that drift in your own life? What does it feel like from the inside?

---

### Chapter Two — The Tree and the Garden

8. The book says the tree in Eden marks two directions: receiving versus taking, participation versus control. Where in your own life have you noticed yourself taking rather than receiving — reaching outward before being rooted?

9. "Trees survive through hidden dependence on what lies beneath the surface." What have you discovered about the hidden things that sustain your own life, in seasons when the surface has failed?

10. The cross is described as the tree of surrender — where grasping is replaced by trust. Does that image resonate emotionally? What does it ask of you personally?

---

### Chapter Three — Stone and Witness

11. "Stone is received. Brick is manufactured." Where in your own life have you noticed yourself preferring what is manufactured and standardized over what is received and irregular?

12. The book describes stones as witnesses — things that remain long after human voices disappear. What "stones" in your own life — places, relationships, moments — have functioned as witness or memorial?

13. "What is built without true foundation cannot endure." What does this sentence make you want to examine — not in society, but in your own life?

---

## PART II — BABEL AND THE RISE OF SYSTEMS (Chapters 4–7)

### Chapter Four — Seed and Field

14. "Nearly everything alive begins unseen." Can you trace a moment in your own life when growth was happening underground that you couldn't see yet? What was it like to look back and recognize it?

15. The parable of the sower is described here not as a story about information, but about receptivity. What is the current condition of your interior field? What is growing there? What is crowding it?

16. "A farmer cannot command harvest instantly. He must move according to seasons." Where in your life are you most tempted to command rather than participate? What would it look like to move according to seasons instead?

---

### Chapter Five — Brick and Empire

17. "Babel survives wherever humanity believes: scale creates meaning, visibility creates identity, accumulation creates peace, or collective power can replace dependence on God." Which of these feels most personally alive in your own experience of modern life?

18. The book says the problem is not building itself, but forgetting. What has modern life caused you to forget? What would it mean to remember again?

19. "The kingdoms of humanity often organize around fear: fear of scarcity, fear of weakness, fear of death, fear of insignificance." Which fear drives you most often? How does it show up in the choices you make?

---

### Chapter Six — Wells and Depths

20. "The tower says: 'We will rise.' The well says: 'We need.'" Which posture do you inhabit more naturally? Which posture do you find yourself more socially rewarded for?

21. The book says crowded souls rarely dig wells. What would it mean for your soul to "dig a well" in your current life — to go deeper rather than move faster?

22. Isaac reopened wells that had been buried. What life-giving things — practices, relationships, ways of seeing — have been buried in your own life that might be worth uncovering?

---

### Chapter Seven — Wilderness and Shepherds

23. The manuscript says wilderness removes distraction and exposes dependence. Has there been a "wilderness season" in your life — a time when ordinary supports disappeared and you were left with something more basic? What did you find there?

24. "The shepherd leaves the ninety-nine to search for one." Does the image of a God who notices individual absence — rather than managing populations — change anything about how you experience your relationship with God?

25. "Formation happens far from spectacle." Where in your own life has the most significant interior work happened? Was it visible to others? Did it feel important at the time?

---

## PART III — TEMPLE, CHRIST, AND FULFILLMENT (Chapters 8–12)

### Chapter Eight — Mountains, Caves, and Lower Places

26. "When humanity encounters true holiness, it does not stand taller. It falls lower." When, if ever, have you experienced something like this — a moment where something real and large made you feel your own smallness, not with fear, but with release?

27. The cave in this chapter functions as a place of descent and preparation before emergence. What "cave" are you in, or have you recently come through? What emerged from it?

28. "The lower place removes false elevation." What false elevations — identities, securities, self-presentations — have been lowered in your life? What did you find underneath them?

---

### Chapter Nine — Temple and Tabernacle

29. The tabernacle moves with the people; the temple is fixed. In your own experience of faith, have you felt more like someone in a tabernacle (moving, dependent, pilgrim) or someone in a temple (settled, institutional, structured)? What are the gifts and losses of each?

30. "Structures can either support communion or replace it." Have you experienced a religious structure — a church, a practice, a tradition — that crossed from support into replacement? What did it feel like to recognize that?

31. "The deepest sanctuary was never merely a building." Where do you actually encounter God most readily? What does that tell you about your own soul's particular language of encounter?

---

### Chapter Ten — The Carpenter and the Cross

32. Christ spent most of His earthly life in obscurity — working with wood, walking dusty roads, largely unseen. What does it mean to you that the majority of the Son of God's life was hidden and ordinary?

33. "At the cross, heaven descends toward humanity" rather than humanity ascending through towers. Does this reversal — the direction of descent rather than ascent — change how you understand what salvation is, or what it feels like?

34. "The stones preach." At the crucifixion, the earth shakes, rocks split, darkness falls — creation itself responds. What is creation saying in that moment, in your reading?

---

### Chapter Eleven — Babel After Babel

35. The book says modern people can be "more connected externally while often feeling more isolated inwardly." Does this describe your own experience? What specific things contribute to this in your life?

36. "Knowledge expands. But wisdom does not necessarily follow." Where in your own life have you seen the gap between knowledge and wisdom — in yourself or in the world around you?

37. "The kingdom grows like roots beneath pavement." Have you seen this — the quiet, persistent life of God working underneath or behind what is visible? What did it look like?

---

### Chapter Twelve — The Language of Creation

38. "He who has ears to hear, let him hear." The book suggests this is about perception, not merely sound. What has helped you develop spiritual perception in your own life? What has obscured it?

39. When was the last time the natural world — weather, a tree, a body of water, a night sky — stopped you in some way? What did it say, if anything?

40. The manuscript says ancient laborers (shepherds, farmers, sailors) perceived truths that modern civilization often overlooks because they lived in intimate dependence on creation. What have you personally lost — or gained — by living further from that dependence?

---

## PART IV — RESTORATION AND THE LIVING FIELD (Chapters 13–18)

### Chapter Thirteen — The Stone the Builders Rejected

41. "The builders reject the stone because the stone cannot be fully controlled." What truths in your own life have you found yourself resisting because they threatened your sense of control?

42. The cornerstone establishes alignment for everything else. What is the cornerstone of your own life — the thing upon which you have actually built your sense of identity and stability? Is it the cornerstone you would choose if you were being honest?

43. "Reality itself still waits patiently in the dust, solid enough to build upon again." This is offered as hope rather than condemnation. Do you receive it as hope? What makes it difficult or easy to receive?

---

### Chapter Fourteen — The Living Field

44. The chapter says the human heart is a field — and whatever you repeatedly receive shapes what grows. Looking at your actual life (not your intentions), what are you regularly planting? What is the crop beginning to appear?

45. "Many people avoid silence because silence allows buried realities to surface: fear, grief, emptiness, longing, fatigue." What surfaces in you when you are quiet? What does that tell you about what you might need?

46. "The soul was not designed for endless consumption. It was designed for rootedness." What does rootedness feel like, in your experience? When have you been most rooted? What made it possible?

---

### Chapter Fifteen — The Harvest and the Laborers

47. "The world is not divided into superior people and inferior people. All are laborers. All are fields. All require rain." Does this posture — seeing people as fields, not problems — change how you see the difficult people in your life?

48. "The laborer is not responsible for controlling the entire harvest. The laborer is responsible for faithfulness." What would change in your daily life if you actually lived from this understanding?

49. "Compassionate enough to love the field rather than condemn it." Who or what is the field in your life that you have been condemning rather than loving?

---

### Chapter Sixteen — Rivers, Wells, and Living Water

50. "Come to the water. Come downward. Come thirsty. Come honest." Which of these four invitations is most difficult for you personally? Why?

51. The book says the soul becomes dehydrated through distraction and accumulation — that people often attempt to quench spiritual thirst through entertainment, achievement, and recognition. What have been your own most common substitutes for living water?

52. Water always flows toward the lowest places. Where in your life — what relationship, practice, or posture — have you experienced something like living water arriving in a lowered place?

---

### Chapter Seventeen — The Final City and the Restored Garden

53. The final city contains both river and tree — garden woven into civilization rather than replacing it. Does this vision of restoration (not escape from the world but renewal within it) resonate with you? What does it ask of how you inhabit the present world?

54. "God will dwell with them." The manuscript treats this as the fulfillment of everything that preceded it. What does it mean to you that the final answer in scripture is not a system, a doctrine, or a program, but presence?

55. "No tower rises in defiance. No empire crushes the weak." What would need to change for the world you currently inhabit to begin resembling this even slightly? What role, if any, do you play in that?

---

### Chapter Eighteen — The God of Sticks and Stones

56. The book ends with an invitation "not [to] upward striving toward self-made transcendence, but downward rootedness into reality, communion, and love." What would this look like practically in your ordinary week?

57. "The sticks and stones matter. Not because they are divine. But because they speak." What ordinary thing in your life has been speaking to you — if you are honest about it — that you have not yet stopped to listen to?

58. The final chapter asks what is life, what is truth, what is worth building, what foundation can endure, what truly satisfies thirst. Without reaching for theological answers — what do your actual choices tell you about how you are currently answering these questions?

---

## CLOSING PROMPTS
*For the end of a final discussion session*

59. What in your life began to feel different — even slightly — while reading this book? What ordinary things became more visible?

60. Where in the book did you feel most slowed down? What does that tell you about yourself?

61. Which symbol remained with you after reading — seed, stone, well, tree, river, wilderness, shepherd, garden, or another? Why do you think that one?

62. If you were to carry one sentence from this book into the next season of your life, what would it be?

63. The book describes a "quiet language of God" that has been speaking through creation all along. Have you heard it? What did it sound like?

---

*This discussion guide is designed for contemplative group settings. Most sessions will work best with 2–4 prompts rather than attempting to move through the entire list. Allow silence. Allow partial answers. Allow questions to remain open.*
