# Professional Book Review
## *The God of Sticks and Stones: Scripture, Creation, and the Quiet Language of God*

---

### OVERVIEW

*The God of Sticks and Stones* resists easy classification. It is not a devotional, not a systematic theology, not a cultural critique, and not a nature essay — yet it carries something of each. What it is, more precisely, is a contemplative meditation on biblical symbolism: an extended, slow-moving exploration of how scripture repeatedly uses ordinary, grounded, created things — seeds, stone, wells, trees, fields, shepherds, rivers — to reveal the shape of the kingdom of God.

The manuscript is organized into eighteen chapters arranged across four parts, tracing a movement from the foundations of creation in Genesis through the restoration imagery of Revelation. But structural description alone does not capture what this book actually does to a reader. What it does is slow you down. And for a reader willing to be slowed, it offers something increasingly rare in Christian publishing: a reading experience that feels continuous with its subject.

---

### PROSE STYLE AND FORM

The book's most distinctive formal feature is its lineation. Paragraphs frequently break into fragments — single words, two-word phrases, three-word lines arranged in a kind of prose-verse that falls somewhere between meditative essay and liturgical chant. This is not a stylistic accident. The form is load-bearing.

Consider a representative passage from Chapter Six:

> *A well begins with lack. No one digs downward without thirst.*
>
> *This is what makes the well such a powerful symbol throughout scripture.*
> *The well is not merely about water.*
> *It is about recognition.*
>
> *Humanity cannot sustain itself.*
>
> *The tower says:*
> *"We will rise."*
>
> *The well says:*
> *"We need."*

The fragmentation enacts the message. The text itself decelerates at precisely the moments it invites the reader to consider dependence, hiddenness, and descent. The white space on the page is not decoration — it is invitation. The reader is asked to breathe, to pause, to receive.

This approach carries risks. In less disciplined hands, such fragmentation can feel like padding, or like a writer stretching thin material across decorative space. Here, however, the fragmentation generally serves. The sparse lines accumulate slowly into theological weight, and the recurrent phrases — "downward before upward," "creation never stopped speaking," "the tower seeks visibility; the well seeks life" — build through repetition into something closer to liturgy than argument.

---

### SYMBOLIC ARCHITECTURE

The manuscript's organizing intelligence is symbolic rather than argumentative. It does not build a case the way a treatise builds a case. Instead, it lays a pattern before the reader and asks whether the reader can see it.

That pattern runs as follows: the biblical world is saturated with created symbols — seeds, trees, water, stone, fields — and these symbols are not incidental to scripture's meaning but constitutive of it. The kingdom of God consistently enters history through humble, overlooked, downward-moving things. Babel consistently represents the opposite impulse: the collective human reach for elevation, uniformity, and manufactured permanence.

The central symbolic contrast of the book — tower versus well — is introduced in Chapter One, developed steadily through the middle chapters, and resolved in Chapter Seventeen's vision of the New Jerusalem, where creation (river, tree, stone) is not abolished but redeemed, not replaced by the city but woven into it.

Secondary symbolic threads include:

- **The descent pattern**: seeds descend before rising; roots go underground before branches expand; wells are dug downward; Christ descends into death before resurrection. The manuscript reads this structural movement into virtually every domain of scripture and creation.

- **Stone versus brick**: natural stone (received, irregular, permanent) contrasted with manufactured brick (standardized, scalable, replaceable) becomes the book's sharpest material symbol for the difference between dependence and self-exaltation.

- **The rejected cornerstone**: Christ's identification with the rejected stone becomes the pivot around which the book's Christology turns — understated, but present throughout.

- **Creation as witness**: The manuscript's theological claim is not pantheism but something more careful — that the created order, without being divine, continuously testifies to the character of its Maker.

---

### THEOLOGICAL CHARACTER

This is a book with a theological argument, even if it refuses to make that argument in conventional theological form. The argument is roughly this: the biblical story traces a consistent movement from Eden (garden, communion, dependence) through Babel and its descendants (tower, accumulation, self-exaltation) toward the New Jerusalem (restored city, restored creation, restored communion). Christ appears at the center of this movement as the one in whom all the symbols converge — cornerstone, vine, bread, living water, good shepherd — and through whom the direction reverses: not humanity ascending to heaven through towers, but heaven descending into humanity through grace.

This is not an original theological framework, but the manuscript's contribution is not novelty. Its contribution is the sustained, meditative attention it brings to the symbolic texture through which these themes move. This is a book less interested in establishing doctrine than in restoring perception. The preface states this directly: "The goal is not merely information. The goal is attentiveness."

The theology is broadly orthodox but deliberately non-denominational. There is no ecclesiology here, no sacramental system, no confessional framework. This is both a strength and a limitation. The strength is accessibility — the manuscript speaks across traditions with unusual ease. The limitation is that the vision of "restored communion" occasionally floats without institutional tethering, and readers seeking clarity about the shape of the community into which this restored perception leads may find the book silent.

---

### STRENGTHS

**The cumulative effect of symbolic repetition.** What might initially feel like redundancy — the same themes circling back across chapters — gradually reveals itself as architecture. By the middle of the book, the reader has developed a genuine symbolic vocabulary. When the cross appears in Chapter Ten as simultaneously tree, altar, judgment, and covenant, the weight of that convergence is earned by what came before.

**The treatment of Babel.** The manuscript's reading of Babel as archetype rather than merely historical event is its most intellectually compelling move. Babel is not simply an ancient story about a failed tower. It is the recurring pattern of human civilization attempting transcendence apart from communion — and the manuscript traces this pattern with patience through the entire biblical narrative and into modernity. The chapter "Babel After Babel" (Chapter Eleven) is among the book's most searching.

**The final vision of Chapter Seventeen.** The book's reading of the New Jerusalem as restoration rather than escape — as the reconciliation of garden and city, creation and civilization — is theologically significant and often underemphasized in popular Christian writing. The manuscript handles this well.

**The prose-poetic form at its best.** When the lineation aligns with genuine emotional weight — as it does in the chapters on stone, on the cross, on the shepherd — the effect is genuinely moving. The form matches the content.

---

### PACING AND READER EXPERIENCE

The book is long for its genre — roughly 24,000 words across eighteen chapters — and the density is relatively consistent throughout. There are few chapters that feel transitional or spare in the sense of giving the reader relief. The meditative pace that is the book's gift is also its primary challenge for sustained reading.

Chapters Eight through Twelve — the central block covering mountains, temple, the cross, and Babel's recurrence — represent the most sustained and dense section of the manuscript. This is where the symbolic and theological weight peaks, and readers who find themselves fatigued mid-book are likely finding it here. The material is important, but the pacing does not vary much to accommodate fatigue.

Part Four (Chapters Thirteen through Eighteen) is the most restorative stretch of the book. The prose remains contemplative but opens progressively toward hope and restoration, and the symbolic threads that have been winding through the earlier chapters begin to gather and resolve. The effect of reading into Part Four after the density of Part Three is noticeable — there is something like air returning.

Chapter Eighteen ("The God of Sticks and Stones") functions as the book's summary and final invitation, and it earns its position. It distills the manuscript's central imagery — stick, stone, seed, field, water, tree — and returns the reader to the same ground where the book began, but with accumulated understanding.

---

### CONTEMPLATIVE TRADITION AND COMPARISON

Readers familiar with the wider contemplative literary tradition will find the manuscript in company with several significant predecessors, though it does not imitate any of them directly.

In its attentiveness to creation as revelation, it shares sensibility with **Wendell Berry** — particularly Berry's essays in *The Art of the Commonplace* and *Life is a Miracle* — though the manuscript is more explicitly theological and less agrarian-polemical than Berry's prose.

In its meditative engagement with biblical imagery and the patient accumulation of symbolic meaning, it recalls **Eugene Peterson's** tone, particularly in works like *Earth and Altar* and *A Long Obedience in the Same Direction*. The unhurried pace, the trust in repetition, the resistance to systematic argument — these are characteristic of Peterson's contemplative register.

The symbolic approach to creation as language — not allegory, not metaphor merely, but something closer to testimony — brings the manuscript into proximity with **George MacDonald's** theological imagination and, more distantly, with **Thomas Merton's** later nature essays.

What distinguishes this manuscript from these predecessors is its structural focus on tracking the same symbolic cluster (tower/well, garden/city, seed/stone) across the full sweep of scripture from Genesis to Revelation. This gives it an unusually integrated symbolic coherence.

---

### WHAT DISTINGUISHES IT FROM STANDARD CHRISTIAN NONFICTION

Most Christian nonfiction operates through argument, evidence, and practical application. Books begin with a problem, build toward a resolution, and close with action steps. *The God of Sticks and Stones* does none of this.

It does not argue toward a conclusion so much as walk toward a recognition. The movement is lateral as much as linear — themes circle back, symbols recur, the same contrast (tower versus well, elevation versus descent) returns across different chapters and different scriptural contexts. This is not carelessness but method.

The manuscript is closer to an extended liturgy than to a conventional theological essay. Its unit of meaning is not the paragraph-with-thesis but the image returned to, looked at from a different angle, and set down quietly again.

For readers conditioned by the pace and structure of conventional Christian publishing, this will initially feel slow. The book asks something different. It does not ask to be consumed efficiently. It asks to be inhabited.

---

### ASSESSMENT

*The God of Sticks and Stones* is a serious and carefully made work. Its symbolic architecture is coherent. Its theological instincts are sound. Its prose-poetic form is genuinely in service of its content — form and message are integrated in a way rare enough to note.

It will not reach everyone. Readers who need argument, resolution, or application may find it evasive. Readers who find its pace too slow, its repetitions too many, or its structure too diffuse may not finish it.

But for readers willing to enter its rhythm — for those who are, as the dedication says, "weary of noise and still hope to hear the quiet things again" — this manuscript offers something close to what it promises: a slow recovery of attentiveness, and a gradual recognition that the language of the kingdom has been running through creation all along, waiting to be heard.
